### [How Complex Systems Fail: A Classic 1998 Essay Still Shapes Failure Engineering](https://how.complexsystems.fail/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

“How Complex Systems Fail,” a 1998 essay reposted on Hacker News, argues that complex systems are inherently hazardous and that failures are normal, emergent phenomena rather than the result of a single discoverable root cause. The piece emphasizes that systems continue operating despite many flaws because of redundancies and human adaptation, and that accident reviews often reveal prior “proto-accidents.” Its central warning is that root cause analysis is misleading when applied to complex systems, because many failures arise from interactions among normal behaviors. The essay remains influential in software operations and resilience engineering, where it is frequently cited in discussions about incident response and failure-tolerant design.

**「Background」** &quot;How Complex Systems Fail&quot; is a 1998 essay by patient safety researcher Richard Cook, widely shared across healthcare, aviation, and software engineering. It argues that complex systems are intrinsically hazardous, heavily defended, and never free from failure; what looks like a root cause is usually the last in a chain of latent conditions and normal system operations. The essay is foundational to resilience engineering and has been cited in discussions of operational failure and safety practices.

**「Impact」** For software engineers, SREs, and operations teams, the essay provides a foundational rationale for failure-injection practices like Chaos Engineering and for moving incident reviews away from single root causes toward systemic resilience.

### [Distributed LLM inference reaches 28 TPS over WAN with speculative decoding and CUDA Graphs](https://www.reddit.com/r/MachineLearning/comments/1vw5ysj/28_tps_on_qwen257b_across_two_separate_cloud/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

ShardFlow, a distributed LLM inference framework that splits HuggingFace transformers across GPU machines, achieved 28.10 TPS peak \(20.31 TPS average\) on Qwen2.5-7B using two Google Cloud T4 nodes in Iowa and Oregon connected through an AWS EC2 TCP relay in Ohio, with about 86ms RTT on public internet. By applying neural speculative decoding with K=8 drafted tokens, the framework commits roughly 4.07 tokens per network round trip instead of one, turning WAN latency from a per-token cost into a per-round cost. Capturing the 0.5B draft model’s forward pass as a CUDA Graph reduced draft latency from 112ms to 25ms by replacing roughly 1,500 Python-launched kernels with a single driver call, after kernel launch overhead had left the GPU idle about 65% of the time. The non-speculative baseline was 4.92 TPS, while eager neural drafting reached 14.3 TPS peak, and the same two-node setup handled Qwen2.5-14B with NF4 4-bit quantization at 14.43 TPS average. The v2.1 fix demonstrates that replacing Python-loop kernel launches with CUDA Graph replay can produce large latency gains in distributed inference.

reddit · r/MachineLearning · /u/katua\_bkl · Aug 23, 12:30

**「Background」** Speculative decoding uses a small draft model to propose several future tokens, then a larger target model verifies them in parallel, accepting a block when the guesses match; this turns network round-trip latency into a per-round cost rather than a per-token cost. CUDA Graphs allow a sequence of GPU kernel launches to be captured and replayed as a single operation, removing per-kernel CPU launch overhead and keeping the GPU busier.

**「Impact」** Practitioners running multi-node LLM inference over public WAN can use speculative decoding plus CUDA Graph replay to amortize round-trip latency, turning roughly 5 TPS into 28 TPS peak on Qwen2.5-7B in ShardFlow&\#x27;s two-region T4 benchmark.

### [Speculative Decoding on AMD GPUs in vLLM: Methods and Measurements](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-08-23-speculative-decoding-amd-gpus) ⭐️ 8.0/10

rss · vLLM Blog · Aug 23, 00:00

**「Background」** Standard autoregressive decoding in LLM serving commits one token per target-model pass, so output-token throughput is limited by token-by-token generation. The authors explore speculative decoding in vLLM on AMD GPUs as a way to reduce the number of target-model decode rounds while preserving the original model&\#x27;s output behavior.

**「Solution」** They explain the draft-and-verify process, where a lightweight draft component proposes candidate tokens and the target model verifies them left-to-right, accepting tokens until the first rejection. The article compares five drafting approaches—native MTP, Gemma 4 MTP, EAGLE-3, DFlash, and DSpark—grouped into native MTP modules, separate MTP drafters, and dedicated target-conditioned draft networks. On AMD Instinct MI300X and MI355X GPUs with ROCm, measured speedups varied widely with proposal length, model family, draft checkpoint, and workload. For example, EAGLE-3 reached roughly 2.2x throughput on some Gemma 4 workloads, while DFlash hit around 2.87x at a proposal length of 7; overall acceptance rate declined as N grew, so larger speculative lengths did not always improve throughput. The post also includes example vLLM serve commands and notes that results depend on system configuration, software, drivers, and vLLM version.

**「Takeaway」** The central lesson is that speculative decoding should be treated as a tunable, workload-specific optimization rather than a guaranteed speedup: users need to benchmark each drafting method and speculative length for their own model and task. The authors provide concrete measurements and configuration guidance to make that evaluation practical on AMD GPUs.
